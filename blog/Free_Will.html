Free Will is Poorly Defined

James Squires • Feb 12, 2026

Many philosophical questions can be summed up as poorly written and poorly defined. An example would be whether one has free will. When we discuss free will, we use "I", or "You" to refer to an individual. What does it mean to be an 'individual'? Do we want to think of the individual as a 'unity'?

Some Common Pitfalls

Definition 1: Determinism

Suppose free will is defined as whether an 'individual' has determinism in their actions. Now preclude the universe is probabilistic. We've achieved a randomness in ones actions. If you wish to define randomness in one's response as free will, then sure, you have succeeded in your case that we have free will.

Definition 2: Agency over Actions

If anyone tries to argue this, I will accuse them of bad logical practice. This just begs the question, what is the definition of 'agency over actions'. I believe we might just have to define a primitive somewhere. Additionally, what exactly is this agent we're referring to? The individual is not, in fact, atomistic in nature. Due we define our agent on the psychological level? The neurological leveL? The memetic level? It seems the whole is greater than the part, but the whole is too large to apply to this property.

A Careful Analysis

Jungian Model and Processes

The Jungian model serves as an effective approximation of the high level abstraction of the human psychology. If we impose archetypal theory, then there is no such thing as a unified conscience-- that is if we choose to view the conscience as an archetype. Even the individuation of an individual is asymptotic at best. But we also know archetypes are simple and linear. The push and pull is what creates interaction. And typically the question of free will of an agent is a property of these interactions. So maybe we want to define free will as some sort of property distributed throughout the system of our interacting components. Furthermore, we want to characterize free will by the interactions between these parts. It is fair to say that we can ascertain ALL the properties about the objects from their cohesive interaction, especially on the mathematical level, even constructively. This is beginning to seem a bit non-sensical. A bit categorical....

The Conscience Approximation

We assume that the aggregation of our archetypes is our conscience and the conscience is approximately the agent.

At this point, we have acknowledged complexity, but we still haven't fully embraced it. We haven't even made free will explicit, rather we have simply framed free will as a categorical problem. I dare say that even if we've defined free will, there is the high likelihood of this 'property', whatever it may be, to not instantiate a dichotomous truth value. Furthermore, the astute reader may have realized that I defined the interaction between archetypes as the thing we wish to measure for free-will, but this isn't quite right. Archetypes push and pull on each other, but what we mean is that archetypes combine as physical events through the body. Thus we may wish to measure free will through the physical manifestation of the person. If we impose this as a constraint, this resolves the problem of measurement of free will into an issue of decidability without the need for any sort of platonist tom foolery; that is, we have made our question witnessable. And of course, the actual physical embodiment has reciprocal influence on our archetypal abstraction, but we don't need to account for this if we simply take the action of our archetypes at any given moment, as our archetypes should already be instantiated with this recursive interplay. With this in mind, it seems that we may want to measure free will through physical events.

To summarize, we have defined our archetypes as the conscience. We have defined free will as a property of the interaction between archetypes. These interactions are manifested in the physical, and we want to measure this somehow. If we measure physical manifestation, then we have obtained information that we can define the free will on.

P.S. I'm doing my best to defend a question that I truly believe is non-sensical, but this is quite the challenge. I need to proof read this.
