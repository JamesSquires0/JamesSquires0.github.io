Free Will is Poorly Defined

James Squires • Feb 12, 2026

Many philosophical questions can be summed up as poorly written and poorly defined. An example would be whether one has free will. When we discuss free will, we use "I", or "You" to refer to an individual. What does it mean to be an 'individual'? Do we want to think of the individual as a 'unity'?

Some Common Pitfalls

Definition 1: Determinism

Suppose free will is defined as whether an 'individual' has determinism in their actions. Now preclude the universe is probabilistic. We've achieved a randomness in ones actions. If you wish to define randomness in one's response as free will, then sure, you have succeeded in your case that we have free will.

Definition 2: Agency over Actions

If anyone tries to argue this, I will accuse them of bad logical practice. This just begs the question, what is the definition of 'agency over actions'. I believe we might just have to define a primitive somewhere. Additionally, what exactly is this agent we're referring to? The individual is not, in fact, atomistic in nature. Due we define our agent on the psychological level? The neurological leveL? The memetic level? It seems the whole is greater than the part, but the whole is too large to apply to this property.

A Careful Analysis

Jungian Model and Processes

The Jungian model serves as an effective approximation of the high level abstraction of the human psychology, and this theory implies there is no such thing as a unified conscience. Even the individuation of an individual is asymptotic at best. But we also know archetypes are simple and linear. The push and pull is what creates interaction. And typically the question of free will of an agent is a property of these actions. It is obvious enough that crediting any partial component of a person as the agent is insufficient. A neural pathway is not the person, nor is their entire brain! Nor their body! Afterall, memetic behavior results from interaction between the body and the environment, including other persons. So maybe we want to define free will as some sort of property distributed throughout the system of our interacting components. Furthermore, we want to characterize free will by the interactions between these parts. It is fair to say that we can ascertain ALL the properties about the objects from their cohesive interaction, especially on the mathematical level, even constructively. This is beginning to seem a bit non-sensical. A bit categorical....

At this point, we have acknowledged complexity, but we still haven't fully embraced it. We haven't even defined free will, rather we have simply framed free will as a categorical problem. I dare say that even if we've defined free will, there is the high likelihood of this 'property', whatever it may be, to not instantiate a dichotomous truth value. Furthermore, the astute reader may have realized that I defined the action between archetypes as the thing we wish to measure for free-will, but this isn't quite right. Archetypes push and pull on each other, but these pushes and pulls are actually actions upon the body. When there is a dynamic, there is a physical exhibition that results from said dynamic. Thus we may wish to measure free-will through the physical manifestation of the person. A person is their deed. And of course, the actual physical embodiment has influence on our archetypes, but we don't need to account for this if we simply take the action of our archetypes at any given moment, as our archetypes should already be instantiated with this recursive interplay. With this in mind, it seems that if our archetypal interactions are full and faithful to their physical manifestations, we may want to measure free-will as an actual physical property?
